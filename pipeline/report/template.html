For the backend

What these runs taught us

Alignment

Request 0.15°, crop to 0.1°

Turning a square cutout north-up leaves empty corners (see M101). For M104 we asked IRSA for 0.15° and reprojected onto the 0.1° grid, and the corners filled completely.

Grouping

Not every visit is a full pass

M104 has a one-day visit in February 2026 with 10 images and no D1 at all. The backend needs a rule for short visits, and the colour image needs to cope with a missing detector (here it turns amber).

Downloads

Each cutout is 4–4.5 MB

M101 came to 628 MB and M104 to 766 MB, mostly the extra layers rather than the image. IRSA also returned temporary 503 errors, so retries are a must. Cache everything and never fetch on page load.

Background

Subtract the ZODI layer

Each cutout includes a model of zodiacal light. Subtracting it before combining gives a flat dark sky between passes.

Edges

Some cutouts are cropped

When the target sat near a detector edge, the cutout came back as small as 40 pixels on one side. Coverage should be tracked per image, and combining with a median handles the gaps.

Colour key

Use measured wavelengths

D1 lands between about 0.74 and 1.11 µm, D3 1.62–2.42 µm and D6 4.41–5.01 µm, depending on where the target falls on the detector. The medians (about 0.9, 2.0 and 4.7 µm) make honest labels.

For brainstorming

Where our spin could come from

Two clocks: within a visit and between passes

Blinking passes six months apart catches slow, distant things. Flipping through exposures inside one visit catches fast, nearby things like asteroids. Showing both timescales could be the app's signature.

Point the chart at the ecliptic

Asteroids crowd near the ecliptic. M104 sits about 7° from it and M45 about 4°, so they're far more likely to show real movers than M101 or M51.

Planet X would jump, not creep

Rough estimate: from Earth's orbit, an object at 500 AU shifts about 14′ over six months, and one at 1,000 AU about 7′. Both are bigger than our 6′ field, so a distant-planet hunt needs wider fields or a search across neighbouring tiles.

A spectrum scrubber

SPHEREx is a spectral survey: every exposure is a slightly different wavelength. A slider that sweeps one target from 0.75 to 5 µm would show stars fading and dust lighting up, something no ordinary sky viewer offers.

Difference mode as a toggle

Next to Play, a Difference toggle would turn the plate into "what changed" and make faint movers pop without any extra data.

Bonus visits as timeline ticks

Short visits like M104's February one could appear as small ticks between the main passes: extra moments to check a candidate, without pretending they're full passes.
